A Brief Historical Overview

The Boston Overseers of the Poor were founded in 1692 and incorporated in 1772. It consisted of twelve Boston selectmen (one for each ward), who were responsible for providing relief for “deserving persons.” This relief came in the form of food, housing, medicine, fuel, and sometimes money, and was (in many cases) reserved for Boston residents. If non-residents sought relief, they would often be “Warned Out” of the city, to assure the city’s residents were those receiving the welfare. The Overseers held authority to send individuals to the almshouse and workhouse at their discretion. The Boston Almshouse began at Beacon St until moving in 1801 to Leverett St, and finally to Deer Island in 1825. State almshouses in Bridgewater and Tewksbury opened in 1854. These spaces were to provide relief through food, housing, fuel, medicine and sometimes money to those in need. In response to the influx of migrants to Boston, the Overseers opened The Temporary Home for Women and Children in 1857 at 36 Charles St. The home quickly became overcrowded, and disease became rampant in these spaces. The Overseers later built a larger shelter in 1869 in New Chardon St in Boston’s West End, named the Charity Building. It’s primary function was to provide food, shelter and healthcare for up to 100 women and children at a time. As reflected in the Records, the women and children who passed through these homes tell stories of heartbreak, joy, desperation, love, and abuse. Catherine Wray, October 1863, (Massachusetts Historical Society) The Home was also responsible for taking in abandoned children, who were often left on nearby doorsteps and brought by police officers to the Home. When taken in, the matron or overseer of the Home would fill out an intake form that recorded their age, where they were found, health status, sex, and other distinguishing features. In some cases, overseers used this information to try and reconnect the child with the parent, or use it to verify qualifications for prospective adoptive parents. Children were found all across the city’s wards, though most were found on the doorsteps of homes in the West End, and close to the Home. Adults who sought to adopt abandoned children came mostly from New England, though some did reside in other areas of the United States, and one was recorded as being adopted to parents in London, England. Parents often were required to provide a recommendation before adopting. The Home cared for women and children of varied nationality, gender, age, and race.
